How to improve your sleep quality, one habit at a time

Good sleep is built during the day as much as at night. These seven habits are widely recommended for people who want to fall asleep more easily and wake up more rested.

Keep a steady wake-up time

Waking at the same time every day, including weekends, helps your body clock settle. Consistency matters more than trying to force an early bedtime.

Get morning light

Daylight within an hour of waking helps set your internal clock, which makes it easier to feel sleepy at night.

Cut back on late caffeine and heavy meals

Caffeine can stay in your system for many hours. Try to stop caffeine in the afternoon and avoid large meals close to bedtime.

Make the bedroom cool, dark and quiet

Use curtains or an eye mask, reduce noise, and keep the room comfortably cool. Reserve the bed for sleep rather than work.

Build a calm wind-down

Spend 30 to 60 minutes on low-key activities such as reading, gentle stretching or a warm shower. Dim the lights and put screens away if they keep you alert.

  • Write tomorrow's to-do list so your mind can switch off.
  • If you cannot sleep after about 20 minutes, get up and do something quiet until you feel sleepy.

Common sleep myths

  • Myth: Alcohol helps you sleep. Fact: It can fragment sleep later in the night.
  • Myth: You can fully catch up at weekends. Fact: A steady schedule works better.
  • Myth: Lying in bed awake is still restful. Fact: It can train your brain to link bed with worry.

Why sleep matters

Sleep supports almost every system in the body.

Memory
During sleep the brain sorts and stores what you learned during the day.
Mood
Short sleep makes stress and irritability harder to manage.
Immunity
Regular sleep supports the body's defences against illness.
Energy and focus
Alertness, reaction time and decision-making all drop when you are tired.

Larks and owls: your chronotype

People differ in when their body clock prefers sleep. Morning types feel alert early and tire early. Evening types peak later in the day. Chronotype is partly genetic and changes with age, so teenagers naturally drift later. Rather than fighting your type, find the most regular schedule you can that fits work and family, and use morning light to nudge your clock earlier if you need to.

Caffeine: how much and how long

Caffeine has a half-life of roughly five hours, though it varies between people. A coffee at 4 pm can leave about half its caffeine in your body at 9 pm.

The ideal bedroom

Four things to set up once.

Cool
Many sleep experts suggest roughly 16 to 19 °C.
Dark
Blackout curtains or an eye mask. Cover small standby lights.
Quiet
Use earplugs or steady background noise if you cannot control sound.
Comfortable
A mattress and pillow that support your usual sleeping position.

When to talk to a doctor

Self-help habits are not enough for every sleep problem. See a doctor or sleep specialist if:

  • You have trouble sleeping at least three nights a week for three months or more.
  • You snore loudly, gasp, or stop breathing during sleep.
  • You feel very sleepy in the daytime despite enough time in bed.
  • You have an urge to move your legs that disrupts sleep.

Frequently asked questions

How many hours of sleep do adults need?

Most adults need around seven to nine hours, though individual needs differ.

When should I see a doctor about sleep?

If you snore loudly, stop breathing at night, or have trouble sleeping for several weeks, speak to a doctor. This guide is general information, not medical advice.
